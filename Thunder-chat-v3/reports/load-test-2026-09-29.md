# How many people can use Thunder at once?

Measured on thunder-main, 2026-09-29 01:05-01:15 EDT. Read-only: nothing was
restarted, no config or env var was changed, and no reply was written to
Thunder's memory or history.

Script: `load-test-2026-09-29.py` in this directory.

## The short answer

**One person at a time gets the fast Thunder. Everybody else stands in line.**

Ollama is running the chat model with **one slot** (`-np 1`), so requests are
served strictly one after another. Two people who hit send at the same moment
do not share the card — the second one waits for the first to finish
completely, then starts from scratch.

The card is fast. The line is the problem.

## Setup that was measured

| | |
|---|---|
| Model | `thunder-gptoss:latest`, 11 GB, 100% on GPU |
| Context | 16384 (`THUNDER_NUM_CTX` unset, so the 16384 default) |
| Ollama | 0.33.3 |
| `OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL` | **not set** — runner launched with `-np 1` |
| `OLLAMA_MAX_QUEUE` | **not set** (default 512) |
| GPU | RTX 3090, 13994 MiB of 24576 MiB used, ~10.3 GB free |

The `-np 1` was read off the live runner process, not assumed:

    /usr/local/lib/ollama/llama-server --model ... -c 16384 -np 1 ...

Requests went straight to Ollama at `127.0.0.1:11434/api/chat`, using the same
model tag and the same `CHAT_OPTIONS` production uses. Each request had a
distinct first line so no two shared a cached prompt. One warm-up request
first. The prompt was "Explain how a medical claim moves from the doctor's
office to payment, in about 250 words."

## Measured results

| N at once | avg time to first word | slowest first word | avg tok/s per user | total tok/s | slowest full reply |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.98s | 0.98s | 131.8 | 129.1 | 3.7s |
| 2 | 9.71s | 11.40s | 129.1 | 126.6 | 14.7s |
| 4 | 9.32s | 21.50s | 131.1 | 128.8 | 23.9s |
| 8 | 21.05s | 41.36s | 130.5 | 128.2 | 44.9s |

No request failed at any N. Reply lengths varied (477-928 tokens average per
run) because the model decides its own length; that is why N=2's average
first-word time looks worse than N=4's — that run happened to draw longer
answers.

**The two flat columns are the finding.** Per-user speed stays at ~130 tok/s no
matter how many people are waiting, and total output never rises above ~128
tok/s. If the card were genuinely serving several people at once, per-user
speed would fall and the total would climb. Neither moves. Everyone gets full
speed — when their turn finally comes.

GPU memory during the 8-user run: **13994 MiB, unchanged from idle**, and
utilization only 47-64%. One slot cannot fill the card.

### The queue, caught in the act

Two simultaneous requests, timestamped from the same instant:

    r1: first hidden reasoning @0.15s   first VISIBLE word @2.04s   done @4.87s
    r0: first hidden reasoning @4.97s   first VISIBLE word @6.10s   done @9.51s

`r0` got nothing at all until `r1` finished at 4.87s. That is a pure FIFO
queue, not sharing.

Also visible here: gpt-oss thinks before it speaks, and `ollama_chat_stream`
in `app.py` forwards only `message.content` and drops `thinking`. So ~2s of
every turn is the model reasoning with nothing on screen. The "first word"
numbers above are what a user actually sees in the app, reasoning delay
included.

**One chat turn occupies the whole GPU for about 4-7 seconds.** That single
number drives everything below.

## Plain English, for the owner

**Measured.** If you are the only person typing, Thunder is quick: first words
in about a second, the whole answer in under 4 seconds. If eight people hit
send at the same second, the unluckiest one stares at nothing for 41 seconds
and waits 45 seconds for a finished answer.

Against the bar of "first words under 3s, answer done under 15s":

| People typing at the same moment | How it feels |
|---|---|
| 1 | Fine. First words ~2s, done ~5s. |
| 2 | Usable. The second person waits ~6s for words, ~10s for the answer. |
| 3 | Borderline. Third in line is right at the 15s edge. |
| 4 or more | Bad. 20s+ of waiting, and it grows with each extra person. |

**Estimated, not measured.** Nobody in an office hits send in the same second
all day. If a turn costs ~5 seconds of GPU and a busy person sends a message
every couple of minutes, each active person uses roughly 4% of the card. Doing
the standard queueing arithmetic on that:

- **about 6 people going back and forth at once** stays comfortable (typical
  wait ~7s)
- **about 12** is the ceiling before waits get annoying (typical wait ~10s,
  with bad moments well past that)

So for a small family billing office where people dip in and out rather than
chatting nonstop, **Thunder as it stands today comfortably serves a handful of
people — call it 5 or 6 regular users, a dozen at a stretch.** What it cannot
do today is serve even two people *simultaneously* without one of them
noticing.

Two honest caveats on those estimates: they come from arithmetic on the
measured 5-seconds-per-turn figure, not from a test with real users. And this
test went straight to Ollama, skipping the API — a real `/chat` turn also does
memory recall through the embedding model (which is *also* running with one
slot), so real turns cost a little more than measured here. The numbers above
are a best case.

## Recommendation only — nothing was changed

Setting **`OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL=4`** is the obvious next thing to try. The
evidence that it would help:

- 10.3 GB of VRAM is sitting free
- the extra memory needed is small — the logs show this model's cache running
  about 24 KiB per token, so a full 16384-token slot is only ~384 MiB, and
  three more slots would cost roughly 1.2 GB
- the GPU is only 47-64% busy even with eight people queued, so there is real
  compute going to waste

Expected effect, **by reasoning and not by measurement**: total throughput
rises and the queue mostly disappears for small groups, while each individual
user gets somewhat slower than 130 tok/s, because four people sharing the card
genuinely do share it.

I did not make this change. It means editing the ollama systemd unit and
restarting the service, which drops the loaded model — not something to do to a
live box without Blayne saying go. **The size of the win is unmeasured; it
should be tested with this same script before and after before anyone believes
a number.**
